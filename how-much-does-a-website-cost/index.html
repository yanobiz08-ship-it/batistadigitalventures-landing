How much does a website cost in the U.S.?

It depends less on “the website” and more on what it has to do and who builds it. Here are the market ranges with their sources, what moves the price, what upkeep costs, and the red flags to watch for before you hire.

Updated: November 2026 · By Yano Batista, Batista Digital Ventures

The short answer

  • With a builder like Wix or Squarespace, the platform runs about $10–$50 a month, but you do the work.[Source 2]
  • A freelancer typically charges $1,500–$8,000 for a business site, and an agency $6,000–$35,000 or more.[Source 1]
  • 61% of small businesses spent under $10,000 on their most recent website (Clutch survey, cited by SiteGround).[Source 1]

What moves the price up or down

The same five-page site can cost about $200 a year if you build it, around $4,000 from a freelancer, and $12,000 or more from an agency: what changes is who does the work.[Source 1] These also matter:

  • Number of pages, and who writes the copy and sources the photos.
  • Custom design or an adapted template.
  • Features: bookings, payments, user accounts, and integrations with other systems.[Source 1]
  • Languages: every version is content to write, review, and maintain.
  • Migrating an existing site without losing your Google rankings (redirects, content, email).
  • Hourly rate: agencies charge $25–$150 an hour depending on region and experience.[Source 2]

How much website maintenance costs

It's the cost many quotes leave out. For a small-business site, SiteGround estimates about $600–$6,000 a year in total, with full-service maintenance plans at $95–$195 a month; for a web app, 15% to 25% of the build cost every year.[Source 1]

  • Domain: $10–$30 a year.[Source 3]
  • Hosting and SSL certificate: SSL is included with most modern platforms and hosts.[Source 3]
  • Updates, backups, security, and small changes.

Red flags before you hire

  • They won't put the scope, stages, and price in writing before starting.
  • They ask for 100% upfront instead of payments in stages.
  • The domain or hosting ends up in the provider's name instead of yours.
  • It's unclear whether you get the code and logins once you've paid.
  • They guarantee you the #1 spot on Google: Google itself warns that no one can guarantee that.[Source 7]
  • The monthly maintenance price only shows up after delivery.

DIY or hire a professional?

Do it yourself if…

  • You're testing an idea or your budget is very tight.
  • You have time to learn the tool, write, and maintain it: what you don't pay in money you pay in hours.[Source 1]
  • A template is enough: Squarespace Basic is $19/mo billed yearly ($25 month to month).[Source 5]

Hire a professional if…

  • Your site has to win customers, not just exist.
  • You need well-built English and Spanish, forms, payments, or integrations.
  • You'd rather spend your hours on your business and have someone to call if something breaks.

Pricing FAQ

How much does a simple small-business website cost?

According to Clutch, a freelancer charges $500–$5,000 for a basic 5–10 page site, and most websites cost under $10,000. At BDV, a 5-page site typically costs $1,500–$3,500.

How much does website maintenance cost per month?

SiteGround estimates full-service maintenance plans for small businesses commonly cost $95–$195 a month, and that a site costs about $600–$6,000 a year in total. Our website maintenance is $99–$249 a month, month-to-month, with no cancellation penalty.

Does a bilingual website cost more?

If you hire translation separately, yes: professional translation in the U.S. usually costs $0.15–$0.30 per word, according to Smartling. Our websites are built in English and Spanish.

Is it cheaper to build it myself with Wix or Squarespace?

In cash, yes: Clutch puts these builders at about $10–$50 a month. But you do the design, copy, photos, and upkeep yourself, so it's worth pricing your own hours too.

Why do quotes vary so much?

Because they rarely price the same thing: pages, features, who writes the content, and who does the work all change. Always ask for the scope in writing so you compare like with like.
